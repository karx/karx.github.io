# LinkedIn Blog

## Post: What the Agent Revolution Is Teaching Us

---

### Audience
AI tinkerers, LinkedIn engagers, tech community leaders, open source builders, 15+ year industry engineers.

---

### Core Thesis
The explosion of AI agents isn't just a technical shift — it's a behavioral one. Here are 5 things this movement is actively teaching us.

---

### Opening Section — Agent vs Skill vs Context (with diagram)

Three terms. One opinionated model.

**Agent** — the runtime entity. Has *agency*: the capacity to attempt, follow through, and loop. Not a function. An entity.

**Skill** — encoded knowledge. How to use tools. How to perceive. How to transform. Crystallized, reusable, static.

**Context** — not a container, not an input. Context is the surface area the agent creates in the run. Each loop expands it. The agent doesn't swim in context — it *builds* it.

> "Context isn't what you give an agent. It's what the agent makes."

**Diagram:** opinionated agent runtime loop — Skill (static, invoked) → Agent (perceive → act → reflect) → Context (expanding surface, each loop grows it).

---

### Top 5 Learnings

**1. Agency is the real unlock**
Agents don't answer — they *act*. They follow through. Watching that modeled something: the courage to just start doing.

**2. Skill-building is the new architecture.**

But here's what nobody is saying loudly enough:

If you've been building a second brain — Obsidian, Roam, Evernote, a well-catalogued Confluence — it just came alive.

The people reaping the fastest returns from agents are the ones who already treated knowledge as infrastructure. Not documents. Not files. Infrastructure.

Orgs with structured wikis. Engineers with linked notes. PKM practitioners who spent years tagging, connecting, cataloguing — and quietly wondered if it was worth it.

It was worth it.

Agents are the runtime their second brain was waiting for.

Here's why: an agent is only as capable as the skills it can reach for. Skills are encoded knowledge — reusable pathways, composable and callable. If your knowledge is flat and unstructured, the agent has nothing to grip. If it's linked, layered, intentional — the agent runs further, faster, deeper.

The second brain practitioners didn't just get organized. They were building skill surfaces before skills were a thing.

The rest of us are catching up.

Knowledge as storage was the old game.
Knowledge as a surface area agents can act on — that's the new one.

**3. Code is free. Ecosystem is hard.**
Generating code costs nothing now. The real work is scaffolding, integration, trust layers — the *around* the code. That's where the craft lives.

**4. Decomposition is a discipline**
Building agents forces atomic thinking. Clean handoffs, clear context boundaries. That rigor spills back into how you think about everything.

**5. Trust through legibility**
You only trust an agent you can see. This is re-teaching us to demand transparency — from AI systems and human ones.

## Final Post Copy — Version B

Most people are building agents.
Fewer are asking what agents are building in us.

After months of building with AI — here are 5 things this revolution is actively teaching me. But first, a model worth having.

——

Three terms. One opinionated take.

——

1. Agency is the real unlock.

Agents went viral for a reason nobody talks about directly.

It's not the capability. It's the attitude.

The spontaneity. The go-getter energy. The thing that just starts — doesn't wait for perfect conditions, doesn't ask for permission, picks up the task and moves. Loops until done.

That quality is what every manager wants in a hire. What every team wishes it had more of. What separates the people who ship from the people who plan.

Agents modelled it. And people responded.

The virality of AI agents is really a mirror. We built a system that embodies the characteristic we most want at our disposal — in our teams, in ourselves. And then we couldn't stop watching it work.

Agency isn't a feature. It's the thing.

——

2. Skill-building is the new architecture.

But here's what nobody is saying loudly enough:

If you've been building a second brain — Obsidian, Roam, Evernote, a well-catalogued Confluence — it just came alive.

The people reaping the fastest returns from agents already treated knowledge as infrastructure. Not documents. Not files. Infrastructure.

Orgs with structured wikis. Engineers with linked notes. PKM practitioners who spent years tagging, connecting, cataloguing — and quietly wondered if it was worth it.

It was worth it.

The rest of us are catching up.

Knowledge as storage was the old game.
Knowledge as a surface area agents can act on — that's the new one.

——

3. Code is free. Ecosystem is hard.

Code generation is effectively free now. That sentence would have sounded absurd five years ago.

But here's what it unlocks: software built for batch size one.

Not for a market segment. Not for a persona. For you — this context, this moment, this need. The dream of truly personalised software is no longer a product roadmap item. It's a prompt away.

Software skills and agency patterns have matured fast. The primitives exist. The models are capable.

What hasn't caught up is the scaffolding — the orchestration, the memory, the trust layers, the failure handling, the management of agents running in the world on your behalf.

That's the engineering problem now. Not writing code. Governing it.

If the Twitter bubble is real — and some of it is — this is the signal worth keeping: the craft has shifted from authoring software to architecting the systems that author software.

The engineer who thrives next isn't the fastest coder. It's the one who can hold the ecosystem together.

——

4. Decomposition is a discipline.

Building agents forces atomic thinking. Clean handoffs. Clear context boundaries.

You cannot build a good agent with fuzzy thinking. The act of breaking work into composable, callable units — precise enough for a machine to execute — sharpens something.

That rigor doesn't stay in the code. It changes how you think about everything.

——

5. You only trust an agent you can see.

[ WITH VISUAL — Langton's Ant ]

We don't just want agents that work. We want to watch them think.

Audit the steps. Trace the inter-agent communication. See where the reasoning branched, where it stalled, where it surprised you. The desire to observe isn't distrust — it's craft.

Look up Langton's Ant.

A single agent. Two rules. Moves across a grid, flips colours, follows its own logic. For thousands of steps it looks like noise. Then — around step 10,000 — a highway appears. A clean, repeating pattern. Emergent. Undesigned. Inevitable.

You only see the highway if you watched the ant move.

This is what's happening inside every agent run.

A prompt is not a command. It's a starting sequence — a seed handed to a model that then generates something nobody fully predicted. The context grows from there, tick by tick, loop by loop. Each action expands the surface area.

The prompt is the ant's starting rules. The context is the grid it colours.

If you can see that growth — trace it, condense it, tune it — you learn what kind of starting sequence produces what kind of emergence. That's the real feedback loop. Not "did the agent succeed?" but "what did this prompt grow into, and how do I write the next one better?"

The engineers who will matter most in this era study the pattern of the run — not just the output.

Legibility isn't a nice-to-have. It's how you get better.
